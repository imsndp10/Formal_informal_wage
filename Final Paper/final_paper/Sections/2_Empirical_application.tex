\section{Empirical application}

\subsection{Data description}

In our analysis, we used second and third waves (NLFS 2008/09 and NLFS 2017/18) of \textit{Nepal Labor Force Survey} administrated by the National Statistics Office (NSO)(formerly known as Central Bureau of Statistics(CBS)) in collaboration with International Labor Organization (ILO) Nepal. Using a multi-stage clustering sampling approach, these survey collects rich information about individual labor pertaining to demographic profiles, labor market indicators, employment and unemployment and migration. NLFS 2008/09 (referred to as NLFS II hereafter) included interviews with 16,000 households, while the NLFS 2017/18 (referred to as NLFS III) expanded to 18,000 households. We restrict our analysis to individuals of 15-65 years who are in full time wage employment defined as individuals who work for 40 hours or more during the week. We did not include agricultural job sector in the sample, in accordance with This resulted in working-age samples of 45,222 individuals in the second round and 47,899 individuals in the third. From this, we classified employed samples comprising 6718 individuals (35\% formal and 65\% informal employment) and 6827 individuals (23\% formal and 77\% informal employment) in the NLFS II and III respectively. 

We use the measure of job-based concept of informal employment within the working-age samples. For this, we consider the non-agricultural sector . We identify employees in the formal employment as those who work in government service, financial public corporation, non-financial public corporation, governmental and non-governmental organisations and private registered financial and non-financial companies, have a permanent employment and their employer pays social security contribution or they receive the benefits of paid leave or get compensation for unused leave. Based on the same, we identify informal employment by exclusion, where we consider those not falling into these category as informal employment. 

The dependent variable in our study is the logarithm of the hourly income. The survey collects information on the usual hours work per week but not the number of weeks worked during a month. We compute hourly income by dividing monthly income by total imputed hourly work per month. Therefore, we compute the monthly hours of work by multiplying the usual hours of work per week to four. The monthly income is net income paid to people. Thus it includes transfers such as pension, social insurance deductions, and taxes as well as extra income like bonuses, overtime. premiums etc.

We investigate the determinants of wage in our study through the lens of examining human capital variables, personal and household, job and employer characteristics. We focus on experience, experience squared, and education as human capital variables, as extensive literature highlights their significant association with wage levels (Becker, 1975; Borjas, 2005; Rosen, 1972; Mincer, 1974), including the number of years with an employer (Burdett and Cole, 2010). Personal and household characteristics such as gender, caste group, marital status, and the number of children are included, given their proven impact on wages (Bardasi and Taylor, 2008; Killewald and Gough, 2013; Becker, 1975). Job characteristics encompass occupation class, vocational training, migration for work, overtime, and total household chores, all identified as influential in previous research (Becker, 1975; Borjas, 2005). Employer characteristics are captured through the job sector by economic sectors, reflecting their significant role in wage determination (Becker, 1975; Borjas, 2005).

\subsection{Labor market characteristics}

The evolution of Nepal's labor market in the last two decade can be traced back to the profound impacts of conflict and economic liberalization. The decade-long civil conflict from 1996 to 2006 left deep scars on the economy, leading to widespread internal displacement and migration, disrupting traditional employment structures, and pushing many into informal labor. Amidst the conflict, the implementation of economic liberalization policies  sought to stabilize and revitalize the economy. These reforms opened Nepal's markets to global competition, encouraging investment in sectors such as manufacturing and services. However, the liberalization process also exposed the labor market to volatility, particularly in small-scale industries that struggled to compete with larger, more efficient enterprises. As a result, while large-scale manufacturing and service sectors began to formalize and demand higher-skilled labor, many workers were absorbed into the expanding informal sector, which offered immediate, albeit less secure, employment opportunities. The interplay of these forces - conflict-induced displacement, liberalization-driven market shifts, and the need for economic recovery - shaped a labor market where formal employment gradually increased in high-skill sectors, while informal employment persisted, especially in lower-skill, labor-intensive industries.

The market service sector saw notable growth during this period, largely driven by urbanization and increased domestic demand. Employment within this sector diversified considerably, with a clear bifurcation in skill levels. High-skilled jobs, often linked with formal employment, became more prevalent in urban centers, particularly within larger enterprises that adhered to formal labor regulations. In contrast, low and medium-skilled jobs, which constitute a significant portion of the sector, remained predominantly informal. This segmentation reflects the sector's dualistic nature, where formal employment offers better wages, job security, and benefits, while informal employment is characterized by precarious working conditions and lower returns.

Non-market services, primarily involving public sector employment, education, and health, experienced a different trajectory. The government's commitment to expanding education and healthcare infrastructure along with decentralised service delivery to local communities translated into a steady increase in formal employment. The establishment of public enterprises and These sectors demand higher skill levels, reflected in the rising educational qualifications and training requirements for jobs. However, the persistence of informal employment within ancillary services, such as maintenance and support roles, underscores the challenges of complete formalization in a developing economy.

The industrial sector, including both manufacturing and construction, presents a complex picture. Manufacturing growth persists on the back of the construction industry, fueled by post-conflict reconstruction and infrastructure development. The construction industry saw a surge in employment opportunities, offering marginally better jobs to workers out of agriculture  with low skills. This sector's low entry barriers and the temporary nature of construction projects contributed to the dominance of informal employment, which typically involves medium to low-skilled labor. In addition, resurgance of large-scale manufacturers, despite fluctuations, managed to sustain formal employment levels, primarily due to regulatory frameworks and the necessity for skilled labor in specialized production processes. On the other hand, small-scale manufacturers, predominantly viz-a-viz forward linkages to construction sector are low skilled jobs due to the premature and unsustainable transformation of employment sectors that lack aggregate productivity and sustain informally in the market.

The development of formal and informal employment within various job sectors from 2008 to 2018 witnessed significant shifts in employment patterns, with distinct patterns of formalization and skill diversification across sectors. This period was crucial in redefining the skill composition and formalization of employment within these sectors. High-skilled jobs, predominantly found in formal employment, are increasingly concentrated in sectors like education, health, public administration and certain segments of manufacturing. These jobs require advanced training and education, aligning with global trends in labor market specialization. Conversely, medium to low-skilled jobs, which are more prevalent in informal employment, are often found in sectors with less stringent regulatory oversight, such as small-scale manufacturing, construction, and market services.

Education sector shows a growth for both the formal and informal employment.
