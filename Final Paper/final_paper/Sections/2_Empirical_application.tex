\section{Empirical application}

\subsection{Data description}

In our analysis, we used second and third waves (NLFS 2008/09 and NLFS 2017/18) of \textit{Nepal Labor Force Survey} administrated by the National Statistics Office (NSO)(formerly known as Central Bureau of Statistics(CBS)) in collaboration with International Labor Organization (ILO) Nepal. Using a multi-stage clustering sampling approach, these survey collects rich information about individual labor pertaining to demographic profiles, labor market indicators, employment and unemployment and migration. NLFS 2008/09 (referred to as NLFS II hereafter) included interviews with 16,000 households, while the NLFS 2017/18 (referred to as NLFS III) expanded to 18,000 households. We restrict our analysis to individuals of 15-65 years who are in full time wage employment defined as individuals who work for 40 hours or more during the week. We did not include agricultural job sector in the sample, in accordance with This resulted in working-age samples of 45,222 individuals in the second round and 47,899 individuals in the third. From this, we classified employed samples comprising 6718 individuals (35\% formal and 65\% informal employment) and 6827 individuals (23\% formal and 77\% informal employment) in the NLFS II and III respectively. 

We use the measure of job-based concept of informal employment within the working-age samples. For this, we consider the non-agricultural sector . We identify employees in the formal employment as those who work in government service, financial public corporation, non-financial public corporation, governmental and non-governmental organisations and private registered financial and non-financial companies, have a permanent employment and their employer pays social security contribution or they receive the benefits of paid leave or get compensation for unused leave. Based on the same, we identify informal employment by exclusion, where we consider those not falling into these category as informal employment. 

The dependent variable in our study is the logarithm of the hourly income. The survey collects information on the usual hours work per week but not the number of weeks worked during a month. We compute hourly income by dividing monthly income by total imputed hourly work per month. Therefore, we compute the monthly hours of work by multiplying the usual hours of work per week to four. The monthly income is net income paid to people. Thus it includes transfers such as pension, social insurance deductions, and taxes as well as extra income like bonuses, overtime. premiums etc.

We investigate the determinants of wage in our study through the lens of examining human capital variables, personal and household, job and employer characteristics. We focus on experience, experience squared, and education as human capital variables, as extensive literature highlights their significant association with wage levels \parencite{Becker1971, Borjas2005} (Rosen, 1972; Mincer, 1974), including the number of years with an employer (Burdett and Cole, 2010). Personal and household characteristics such as gender, caste group, marital status, and the number of children are included, given their proven impact on wages (Bardasi and Taylor, 2008; Killewald and Gough, 2013; Becker, 1975). Job characteristics encompass occupation class, vocational training, migration for work, overtime, and total household chores, all identified as influential in previous research (Becker, 1975; Borjas, 2005). Employer characteristics are captured through the job sector by economic sectors, reflecting their significant role in wage determination \parencite{Becker1971, Borjas2005}.

\subsection{Labor market characteristics}
The evolution of Nepal's labor market over the last decade has been deeply influenced by its socio-political and economic context. A combination of conflict, liberalization, and structural shifts within the economy has shaped the dynamics of both formal and informal employment, leaving a lasting imprint on the country's labor market. 

The early 1990s also saw a distinct shift in economic policy as Nepal embarked on a program of far-reaching structural adjustment  Deraniyagala2005 \parencite{Bulmer2020} Islam2014. The government initiated liberalization policies aimed at revitalizing the economy, opening Nepal to global markets and encouraging investment in sectors such as manufacturing and services in 1990. It was during this period of economic and political transformation that violent civil conﬂict broke out in Nepal . From 1996 to 2006, Nepal experienced a decade-long civil conflict that disrupted traditional employment patterns, displacing populations and intensifying migration, both internally and abroad. By the early 2000s, Nepal's labor market exhibited the characteristics of a developing economy in the midst of an incomplete structural transformation. Despite some growth in agriculture and services, manufacturing—a sector typically expected to drive industrialization and higher productivity—remained stagnant.  This period of upheaval significantly expanded informal labor as displaced workers struggled to find secure jobs, and the formal labor market contracted under the strain of economic and social instability. As a result, the informal economy grew, absorbing a large portion of workers who faced limited opportunities in the formal sector. 

The decline in manufacturing output, evident even before Nepal's accession to the World Trade Organization (WTO), underscored structural challenges within the sector. Employment in manufacturing increased only marginally during this period, rising from 5.84\% to 6.5\% between 1998 and 2008, a modest gain that fell short of transforming the economy. In the decade of our study, while the manufacturing sector created opportunities for higher-skilled labor in emerging industries, they also exposed small-scale industries to competitive pressures, leading many to struggle or collapse.  Consequently, larger manufacturers, driven by international standards and market competition, increased formal employment, but informal employment continued to dominate, especially in low-skill and labor-intensive sectors where smaller enterprises remained deeply embedded in informal labor markets due to low productivity and regulatory evasion. Those with lower educational attainment were concentrated in informal jobs within small-scale manufacturing, construction, and market services, where wages were higher than in agriculture but did not necessarily reflect higher productivity. Manufacturing saw fluctuations in formal employment due to the uneven performance of large-scale versus small-scale industries. 


The industrial sector, particularly construction, provided an interesting case of growth amidst informality. The post-conflict reconstruction efforts and a surge in infrastructure projects created significant employment opportunities, drawing workers from agriculture into better-paying, albeit temporary, construction jobs. Furthermore, the increased mechanization of construction projects began to erode labor intensity, reducing the sector's capacity to absorb low-skilled workers. The persistence of informality, especially in sectors like construction, was tied to the nature of job creation during this period. Moreover, economic growth during the period of 2008 to 2018 was bolstered by post-earthquake reconstruction efforts, political stability, and remittances from abroad, led to significant real wage increases across both formal and informal sectors. Yet, the creation of new jobs remained uneven across sectors, with the bulk of new employment concentrated in lower-productivity industries such as construction, hospitality, and small-scale manufacturing. They remained largely informal, with minimal job security and benefits. Post-earthquake reconstruction fueled a boom in temporary wage employment, particularly for men, while women were more likely to find work often in agriculture or informal health and education services. As a result, the informal jobs in construction sector grew from close to 30\% in 2008 to close to 41\% in 2018  In construction sectors informal employment remained dominant. Construction, fueled by post-earthquake reconstruction, provided many informal, low-skilled jobs. Despite their growth, these sectors remained informal havens for a large portion of the labor force.


The service sector, meanwhile, grew in prominence, largely fueled by urbanization and rising domestic demand. Yet, the sector exhibited a dualistic nature, with formal, high-skill employment growing in urban centers and larger enterprises, while low and medium-skill jobs remained largely informal. The expansion of informal jobs in the service sector, particularly in retail, transport, and hospitality, provided immediate employment opportunities but also reinforced precarious working conditions, with little to no social protection or benefits. As a result, the informal sector became a cornerstone of Nepal's labor market, characterized by low wages and limited job security.

Non market services Public sector employment, particularly in education, health, and local government services, followed a different trajectory. Government efforts to decentralize services and expand infrastructure in these sectors contributed to steady growth in formal employment, particularly in higher-skilled positions that required educational qualifications and specialized training. However, even in these sectors, the persistence of informal roles in ancillary services, such as maintenance and support functions, highlighted the challenges of achieving full formalization in a developing economy like Nepal. Education emerged as a key driver of wage differentials across sectors, with substantial returns to higher education. Workers with tertiary education were more likely to secure formal employment in higher-paying sectors like finance, public administration, and education. 

Market services sectors such as finance \& insurance, public administration, education, and health \& social work experienced substantial formalization. These sectors benefited from increasing regulatory oversight, higher skill demands, and public sector expansion, particularly in health and education. The rising educational qualifications required for jobs in these industries supported the transition from informal to formal employment, aligning with global trends of formalizing high-skilled sectors. market services sectors such as trade \& repair, and food \& accommodation, informal employment remained dominant. Trade and repair, as well as food and accommodation, saw minimal formalization due to the nature of small-scale, often family-run businesses that operate outside formal regulatory frameworks. Despite their growth, these sectors remained informal havens for a large portion of the labor force.

Market services sectors such as arts, entertainment and other services and real estate had limited formalization during this period. Jobs in these sectors, often part-time or gig-based, remained predominantly informal due to the nature of their contractual arrangements and the irregularity of work. Real estate, though growing, saw informal employment dominate, particularly in construction-linked roles. Market services such as transport \& storage, and professional services exhibited both formal and informal employment. Transport and storage, while offering formal jobs in logistics and urban transport systems, continued to rely on informal labor, especially in rural areas and low-capacity transport services.

The evolution of formal and informal employment in Nepal from 2008 to 2018 reveals distinct patterns of occupational and industry segmentation, providing insights into how varying skill levels and sectors have shaped the labor market. During this period, formal employment became more concentrated in high-skilled jobs, particularly in sectors like finance, health, and public administration, which benefitted from increased regulation and demand for skilled labor. However, medium-skilled jobs, especially in industries such as construction, trade, and food services, remained largely informal. These sectors, characterized by low entry barriers and often limited oversight, continued to rely heavily on informal labor, offering limited job security and benefits. Similarly, elementary jobs—typically low-skill roles in sectors like agriculture, construction, and small-scale services—saw minimal formalization, reflecting the broader difficulties of transforming a developing economy with limited industrialization and weak regulatory enforcement.

High-skilled jobs includes the armed forces, managers, technicians, and professionals. Over the decade, there was a noticeable shift in high-skilled jobs towards greater formalization, especially in professional and managerial roles. Technicians and professionals saw increased formal employment, reflecting the growing demand for skilled labor in sectors like education, health, and public administration. The armed forces, while classified as high-skilled, maintained a significant formal presence as well.

Medium-skilled jobs include clerical support, service and sales, craft and trades, agricultural and forestry, and plant operators. Medium-skilled jobs exhibited a more complex transition between formal and informal employment. Sectors such as service and sales, and craft and trades, continued to rely heavily on informal employment, even as demand grew in urban areas and within small-scale industries. Agricultural and forestry jobs, traditionally informal, showed minimal formalization. Plant operator jobs saw some formalization, especially in sectors like manufacturing and construction, where regulatory pressures began to influence employment contracts. 

Low-skilled jobs includes elementary occupations; roles requiring the least formal education and training, saw the most persistent informality across the decade. This group, comprising a significant portion of the labor force, found informal employment predominantly with little change towards formalization. Informality in this category is often driven by the transient nature of these jobs, which are typically seasonal and lack contractual stability.
