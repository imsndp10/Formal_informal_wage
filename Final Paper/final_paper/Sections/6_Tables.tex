\section{Conclusion}

This paper investigates the structural contributions to wage inequality dynamics between formal and informal employment in Nepal from 2008 to 2018 using the unconditional quantile RIF-Oaxaca decomposition, without inferring any causal effects. Our analysis reveals that in 2008, wage disparity for managerial occupations exhibited an increasing trend across quantiles, while elementary occupations showed a relatively stable and slightly decreasing pattern. This dichotomy resulted in an average wage disparity trend for the overall group, mirrored by the clerical occupation. By 2018, the wage disparity for managerial occupations increased up to the median, while it decreased for elementary occupations, contributing to an average pattern for the overall wage disparity, again reflected by the clerical occupation. The wage disparity between formal and informal employment groups increased across quantiles in both years, with a more pronounced disparity at higher quantiles in 2008 compared to 2018, indicating a convergence in wage structures over the decade. 

Additionally, our investigation into the education and gender dynamics revealed that female participation is more pronounced in managerial occupations compared to elementary ones, and somewhat closer to managerial than elementary for clerical groups. Even with minimal improvement and reduction in gap in average years of schooling from 2008 to 2018 across all the occupation, this trend mirrors the distribution of household chores across occupation groups, indicating that women face a dual burden affecting their market participation flexibility. Women tend to follow the off-the-job benefits of informal employment to balance home and work responsibilities, as evidenced by the increasing unexplained components for females above the median. 

The counterfactual wage distribution, showing hypothetical returns for informal workers as if they were formal, exhibited an increasing trend in both years, highlighting persistent differences in returns to characteristics. The explained component of wage disparity, due to differences in worker characteristics, remained stable across quantiles and years, while the unexplained component, reflecting differences in returns, showed minor fluctuations, indicating persistent structural differences in how characteristics are rewarded. Occupational group analysis revealed that informal labor market discrimination favored formal workers among clerical and managerial occupations, though this decreased from 2008 to 2018. For elementary workers, earnings favored informal workers below the 30th percentile in 2018. 

Our study also examined the sticky floor and glass ceiling phenomena in the informal labor market in Nepal, assessing wage disparity across the earnings distribution and evaluating the contributions of different factors to these disparities. Our findings show that, overall, the wage disparity show the bottleneck effect, with pronounced gap at the median. In 2008, the wage gap was consistent across the distribution, except at the 90th percentile. By 2018, the situation had improved, with the wage gap decreasing below and above the median but remaining at the median. This improvement over the decade is partly due to the managerial occupation having gaps up to the median and clerical occupations showing a wage gap at the median in 2018. Despite contradictory existing literature, our evidence suggests that returns to informal labor market experience have significantly mitigated the earnings gap over the decade, particularly at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles. Our findings emphasize the need for targeted policies addressing structural and discriminatory factors in wage disparities, offering valuable insights for policymakers and researchers. Future research should focus on identifying specific interventions to reduce wage disparities and exploring causal relationships in other developing economies for a more comprehensive understanding of global labor market inequalities.
