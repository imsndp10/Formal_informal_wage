\section{Conclusion}

This paper investigates whether wage disparities between formal and informal employment in Nepal from 2008 to 2018 stem from market segmentation or voluntary choices within a competitive market framework. Using unconditional quantile RIF-Oaxaca decomposition, we found that wage disparity patterns vary across occupations and quantiles, highlighting the complex nature of informal employment.

In 2008, managerial occupations exhibited increasing wage disparity across quantiles, while elementary occupations showed a more stable pattern. By 2018, wage disparities for managerial roles increased up to the median, while they decreased for elementary roles, suggesting a convergence in wage structures over the decade. These findings align with dual market theory, where informal employment often results from barriers to entry into the formal sector, yet also reflect the voluntary choice perspective as some workers benefit from the flexibility and non-wage advantages of informal employment.

Counterfactual wage distribution analysis highlights persistent differences in returns to characteristics between formal and informal sectors. While the explained component of wage disparity remained stable, the unexplained component showed minor fluctuations, indicating ongoing structural differences in how worker characteristics are rewarded. Informal labor market discrimination favored formal workers in clerical and managerial roles, although this decreased over time. For elementary workers, informal employment provided earnings advantages at lower wage levels in 2018.

Examining the sticky floor and glass ceiling phenomena, we found that wage disparities were most pronounced at the median showing a more bottleneck effect. By 2018, the wage gap had decreased below and above the median but remained significant at the median, reflecting improved but persistent inequalities.

Overall, our findings suggest that while informal employment in Nepal is partly a result of labor market segmentation, it also offers voluntary benefits that attract certain workers. This duality underscores the need for targeted policies addressing both structural barriers and the inherent advantages of informal employment. Future research should explore specific interventions to reduce wage disparities and investigate causal relationships in other developing economies for a comprehensive understanding of global labor market inequalities.
