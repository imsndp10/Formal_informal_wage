\subsection{Fixed Effects}

The fixed effects models presented in Tables 8, 9, 10, and 11 examine the determinants of formal employment across different occupational categories: overall, managers, elementary occupations, and clerical occupations. The dependent variable is formal employment, indicating whether an individual is formally employed. Independent variables include household characteristics such as the number of formal members, dependency ratio, work experience, squared experience, and years of schooling. The models control for fixed effects, including gender, urban residence, caste group, and district.

In the overall model (Table 8), the presence of formal members in the household is positively significant across all models for both 2008 and 2018, with increasing returns in 2018 compared to 2008. This suggests that having formal members in the household significantly boosts the likelihood of formal employment, and this effect has strengthened over time. Years of schooling is also consistently significant in all models and years, though its impact slightly diminishes in 2018, indicating a persistent yet slightly weakening influence of education on formal employment.

For managers (Table 9), the presence of formal members remains positively significant in both years, reflecting an enhanced positive correlation between household formal members and formal employment in managerial roles. Interestingly, years of schooling is significant in 2008 but not in 2018, suggesting that the importance of education for managerial positions has decreased over the decade. This indicates that barriers to formal employment due to educational attainment are less pronounced for managers, pointing to a labor market where managerial roles are less influenced by educational credentials compared to other occupations.

In elementary occupations (Table 10), the significance of formal members in the basic model disappears when fixed effects are added, indicating that other fixed characteristics, such as gender, urban residence, caste, and district, play a more critical role in these jobs. The dependency ratio is not significant in 2008 but becomes significantly negative in 2018 when fixed effects are included, highlighting an increased burden of dependents on formal employment in elementary occupations over time. Years of schooling is significant in all models for 2008 but becomes insignificant in 2018 with the inclusion of fixed effects, suggesting that other factors have become more influential in securing formal employment in these roles.

For clerical occupations (Table 11), the presence of formal members is significant in all models for 2008 but only in the basic model for 2018, becoming insignificant with fixed effects. This indicates that the influence of household formal employment status is reduced when other fixed characteristics are considered. The dependency ratio is insignificant in all models for 2008 and becomes significant in the basic model for 2018 but turns insignificant with fixed effects. Years of schooling remains significant across all models and years, though the significance level slightly diminishes in 2018, reflecting the ongoing but slightly weakening importance of education in clerical jobs.

The findings align with Nepal's socio-cultural context, where family decisions are often made collectively. This collective decision-making process can lead to a higher likelihood of multiple household members being formally employed, reinforcing the positive correlation between formal members and individual formal employment status. The dual market in the labor market, as evidenced by the varying significance of years of schooling across occupations, suggests that managerial roles are less constrained by educational barriers, whereas elementary and clerical occupations face more significant educational hurdles.

Overall, the presence of formal household members consistently enhances formal employment prospects, though its impact varies with fixed effects and across occupations. Education remains a crucial factor, though its significance has diminished in certain roles over time. The dependency ratio negatively affects formal employment, with a more pronounced impact in recent years. These findings highlight the evolving dynamics of the labor market and the varying influences of household characteristics, experience, and education on formal employment across different occupational categories, reflecting both the socio-cultural context and the structural dualities within the labor market.

 \subsection{RIF-OLS Decomposition}

Tables 12, 13, and 14 report the estimates of RIF-OLS decomposition and present the baseline results for managers, clerical workers, and elementary workers respectively, with annual normalized wage earnings for 2008 and 2018 as the dependent variable. We report results for the total effect, unexplained and explained effects along with their estimates, standard errors, and bounds. The results indicate that informal employees suffer an earnings penalty across the unconditional earnings distributions for managers and clerical occupations for both 2008 and 2018. For elementary occupations, there is a wage premium for informal employees up to the 30th quantile, whereas a wage penalty exists above the 30th quantile in 2018 and across the distribution in 2008. 

The results show that the raw earnings gap is decreasing for all occupations from 2008 to 2018 across all unconditional distributions, except for the 30-50th quantile for managers. This indicates a convergence in wage structures over the decade, reflecting a narrowing gap between formal and informal employment.

We plot these results as well as the overall wage gap and composition and endowment effects in Figure 3. The results show that the wage gap for elementary occupations is highest at the 10th quantile, starts decreasing, and increases after the median in 2008, whereas it is highest at the 80th quantile, negative below the 30th quantile, and minimal up to the 60th quantile in 2018. For clerical occupations, the wage gap is highest at the 50th quantile, decreases above the median, but decreases at an increasing rate below the median and increases at the 10th quantile in 2008, whereas it is highest at the 40th and 50th quantiles and decreases below and above the median in 2018. This is evidence of the presence of the sticky floor phenomenon in the informal labor market for elementary occupations but not for the rest of the occupations.

\subsection{Discussion}

Our decomposition results align with the literature showing a wage premium for formal employment. Lukiyanova (2013) found that wage inequality was higher in the informal sector in Russia, explained by larger within-sector differences in working hours and a higher dispersion of high earnings. This is consistent with our findings where wage disparity for managerial occupations exhibited an increasing trend across quantiles in 2008, contrasting with the relatively stable and slightly decreasing pattern for elementary occupations.

Wirba et al. (2021) in Cameroon found evidence of a sticky floor phenomenon in the informal labor market, although segment-specific decomposition did not show a compelling sticky floor or glass ceiling effect. Our study supports this by showing wage disparity patterns between formal and informal employment groups, which increased across quantiles in both 2008 and 2018, with a more pronounced disparity at higher quantiles in 2008. This indicates a convergence in wage structures over the decade.

Agyire-Tettey et al. (2017) employed unconditional quantile regressions based on the recentered influence function in Ghana, revealing a sticky floor effect in the formal sector. Our analysis of Nepal indicates that the wage disparity between formal and informal groups was consistent across quantiles, with higher disparity at the top in 2008 compared to 2018, suggesting similar dynamics.

Baum et al. (2024) used RIF quantile regressions to explain disparities between native-born workers and immigrants, while Mattias et al. (2021) used RIF decomposition to study the wealth gap between migrant and native households. Our findings echo these studies, showing that wage disparity for managerial occupations increased up to the median, whereas it decreased for elementary occupations by 2018. This contributed to the average pattern for overall wage disparity, reflected by clerical occupations.

Luca and Marta (2021) revealed that managers and professionals have positive returns on labor incomes, while elementary and service workers show negative associations at the bottom 10\%. In our study, the counterfactual wage distribution showed persistent differences in returns to characteristics, with the explained component of wage disparity due to worker characteristics remaining stable across quantiles and years. The unexplained component, reflecting differences in returns, showed minor fluctuations, indicating persistent structural differences in how characteristics are rewarded.
