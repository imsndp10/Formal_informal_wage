\section{Introduction}

A critical question in understanding the labor market and informing policy recommendations is whether the observed differences in earnings between formal and informal employment result from market segmentation or incentives within a competitive market framework. Do individuals opt for informal employment by choice, or are they pushed into it due to barriers to entry in the formal sector? This question is particularly important in developing economies, where informality is widespread, yet no consensus has been achieved on the driving mechanisms behind informal employment.

Traditional dual market theory, beginning with Lewis (1954), conceives informal employment as a backward, marginal, and disadvantaged sector where employees are paid wages above market-clearing prices and use it as a last resort to escape unemployment (Harris and Todaro, 1970; Fields, 1990, 2005a; Stiglitz, 1976). According to this approach, workers in the informal sector are predominantly involuntarily engaged and earn less than identical workers in the formal sector. This segmentation may result from access to information, geographic barriers, or sector-specific variations in employment and wage regulations (Dickens and Lang, 1992).

However, this notion was challenged by the International Labour Organization (ILO, 1973), which viewed the informal sector as a permanent, economically sufficient, and profit-making small-scale urban economic continuum of employment and income, with little evidence of sectoral mobility (Sethuraman, 1981). This perspective suggests that informal employment results from workers' voluntary choices within a competitive market framework (Maloney, 1999; Rosenzweig, 1988). Workers may trade pecuniary rewards for non-pecuniary benefits, maximizing utility rather than earnings (Maloney, 2004), self-select into jobs based on comparative advantage (Roy, 1951), or seek to avoid tax burdens and inefficient regulations (Fields, 2003).

These contrasting theories lead to the central inquiry: Is informal employment a survival strategy for those excluded from formal jobs or a voluntary choice by workers to maximize their utility? While wage differentials between formal and informal employment are well-documented (Mazumdar, 1975; Pradhan and Van Soest, 1995; Badaoui et al., 2008; Blunch, 2015), lower wages and returns to education in informal employment do not necessarily imply market segmentation if there is mobility between sectors (Gunther and Launov, 2012). Thus, it is necessary to account for unobservable worker characteristics influencing sector choices to distinguish between segmentation and voluntary employment.

The empirical literature predominantly assumes a homogeneous informal sector, but theoretical studies propose a heterogeneous informal sector with two distinct tiers: the lower-tier and upper-tier (Fields, 1990). The lower-tier aligns with the dual market model, while the upper-tier comprises individuals voluntarily choosing informal employment for higher earnings and non-wage benefits (Cunningham and Maloney, 2001; Henley et al., 2009).

This paper aims to examine the impact of informality on earnings for formal and informal sector employees in Nepal, considering unobserved characteristics. Using data from the Nepal Labor Force Survey rounds II (2008) and III (2018), we analyze wage differences among full-time wage employees across three occupational tiers: elementary, clerical, and managerial. By applying linear Recentered Influence Function (RIF)-OLS regressions, we estimate Mincer-type wage equations for different occupations, allowing for detailed decomposition and interpretation of coefficient estimates.

Our findings reveal a dual market structure in Nepal, with a consistent wage penalty for informal workers across occupations in 2008. Notably, informal employees in clerical, elementary, and managerial roles earn less than their formal counterparts, even after controlling for nonlinearity. However, a wage premium exists for informal employment in elementary occupations up to the 40th quantile, likely due to the scarcity of formal jobs. The wage gap for informal clerical and managerial workers is more pronounced beyond the 40th and 20th quantiles, respectively. Over time, the informal wage penalty diminishes, indicating wage convergence. Overall, our study shows that while a persistent wage penalty exists for informal workers, this gap is narrowing, suggesting improvements in labor market dynamics. The results provide insights into wage discrimination and the heterogeneous nature of the informal sector, contributing to a better understanding of labor market dynamics and welfare distribution in developing economies.
