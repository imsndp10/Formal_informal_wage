\section{Introduction}

A critical question in understanding the labor market and informing policy recommendations is whether the observed differences in earnings between formal and informal employment result from market segmentation or incentives within a competitive market framework. Do individuals opt for informal employment by choice, or are they pushed into it due to barriers to entry in the formal employment? This question is particularly important in developing economies, where informality is widespread, interwoven dichotomy between the formal and informal employment is fuzzy and no consensus has been achieved on the contradictory driving mechanisms behind informal employment.

In the 1950s and 1960s, modernization theorists labeled small-scale, unregistered enterprises in developing countries as the ``traditional sector'' \parencite{Lewis1954}, viewing them as primitive, stagnant, and disadvantaged. Workers were thought to earn above-market wages and use these jobs as a last resort \parencite{Harris1970, Fields1990, Fields2005} . Optimism existed that these sectors would modernize. However, an ILO study in Ghana,  \textcite{Hart1973} revealed that unregistered jobs were stable, economically viable, and profit-making, with minimal sectoral mobility \parencite{Sethuraman1981}, prompting the term change to ``informal sector''.

This new term challenged the view of the traditional sector as temporary but retained the dual economy assumption \parencite{Rosaldo2021}. Influential perspectives on informality emerged: the Neoliberal school saw informal workers as entrepreneurial, avoiding restrictive regulations \parencite{desoto1989} , while the Structuralist school viewed them as exploited laborers capable of collective resistance \parencite{Portes1989, Rosaldo2016}. The term ``informality'' evolved from a lack of form and bureaucratic sophistication to lack of regulation and low-income, self-employed urban workers in the Global South, underscoring the need for better recognition and resources  \parencite{Rosaldo2021}. Studying the diversity of informal employment is therefore crucial for understanding informal-formal linkages, potential discrimination, and labor market dynamics in developing countries.

These contrasting theories and diversity of informal employment led to the central inquiry: Is informal employment a survival strategy for those excluded from formal jobs or a voluntary choice by workers to maximize their utility? While wage differentials between formal and informal employment are well-documented \parencite{Mazumdar1976, Pradhan1995, ElBadaoui2008, Blunch2015}, lower wages and returns to education in the informal sector do not necessarily indicate market segmentation if there is sectoral mobility \parencite{Guenther2012} . Thus, it is necessary to account for unobservable worker characteristics influencing sector choices to distinguish between segmentation and voluntary employment.

While empirical literature often assumes a homogeneous informal sector, theoretical studies suggest a heterogeneous sector with two tiers: lower-tier and upper-tier \parencite{Fields1990}. The lower\-tier aligns with the dual market model, where workers face limited opportunities, while the upper-tier includes those who choose informal employment for higher aearnings and non-wage benefits \parencite{Cunningham2001, henley_defining_2009}. Research shows significant heterogeneity in informal earnings \parencite{ Radchenko2016, ABLAZA2021, Garcia2017}. Although formal jobs typically offer better incomes and conditions, this is not always true \parencite{Tucker2020} . Consequently, workers may combine formal and informal employment or choose the latter \parencite{Hunt2009, Purser2009}. This perspective suggests informal employment arises from voluntary choices in a competitive market \parencite{ Rosenzweig1988}. Workers might trade monetary rewards for non-monetary benefits to maximize utility \parencite{Maloney2004}, select jobs based on comparative advantage \parencite{A.D.1951}, or avoid taxes and regulations \parencite{Fields2003} . From an empirical stance, the notion of market segmentation and heterogeneity is still up for a discourse.

This paper provides new evidence on the heterogeneity of the informal employment in Nepal. The novelty of this study is twofold. First, we analyze the informal employment in Nepal, which provides rich evidence from a large and heterogeneous informal employment, that provides a particularly interesting case for analyzing heterogeneity in the informal employment in developing economies.  The existing literature provides inconclusive and mixed reports on the heterogeneity of the informal
employment; this paper uses the case of Nepal to offer more evidence on the issue.  Nepal's shift from an agrarian economy in the 1990s, driven by liberalization and the rise of service and garment industries, was disrupted by a decade-long armed conflict (1996-2006), triggering significant internal and external migration. Post-conflict political upheaval in 2010 and government-led infrastructure investments spurred growth in construction and manufacturing by 2008. However, by 2018, the growth of labor market remained largely informal, hindered by limited formal institutions, fixed government jobs, entrepot trade dominance, ongoing political instability, and natural disasters. Against this backdrop, we analyze the factors that affect wage differentials between formal and informal workers using unconditional quantile decomposition  methodology, controlling for selectivity bias caused by correlated unobserved heterogeneity affecting wages.   

Our second contribution is to conduct the analysis of formal-informal wage gap decomposition 
at skills levels of a occupation group (low, medium \& high skills) using data from the Nepal Labor Force Survey rounds II (2008) and III (2018). The extant literature examined the  influence of gender \parencite{Chen2001, Chen2004, Sethuraman1998, yahmed_formal_2016} , race \parencite{LoukaitouSideris2014}, ethnicity \parencite{HOM2018} , caste \parencite{Agarwala2013} , and regional differences \parencite{Garcia2017}, but doesnot take into account the possibility of occupational skill levels based heterogeneities among informal workers. By applying linear Recentered Influence Function (RIF)-OLS regressions, we estimate Mincer-type wage equations for different occupations, allowing for detailed decomposition and interpretation of coefficient estimates.

Our findings reveal a  market segmentation in informal employment of Nepal, where employees in the low skills jobs are being pushed into it because of their returns to the human capital and market structure, whereas employees in the medium and high skill levels opt for informal employment with choice to maximize their comparative advantage.  Our study also shows a consistent wage penalty for informal workers across occupations in 2008 abeit showing wage  gap convergence over time in 2018. Notably, informal employees in each of skill levels group earn less than their formal counterparts, even after controlling for nonlinearity except for low skill groups upto 40th quantile, most likely because of scarcity of formal jobs being offered to low skill employees.  The wage gap for informal medium and high skilled workers is more pronounced beyond the 40th and 20th quantiles, respectively. Overall, our study shows that while a persistent wage penalty exists for informal workers, this gap is narrowing, suggesting improvements in labor market dynamics. The results provide insights into wage discrimination and the heterogeneous nature of the informal sector, contributing to a better understanding of labor market dynamics and welfare distribution in developing economies.

Finally, Section 5 concludes the paper with potential avenues for further research.
